Symmetry & Tessellation

A friendly, hands-on tour of the maths behind the game: what a tessellation is, the symmetries a tile can have, lattices, holohedry, and how a pattern gets its wallpaper-group name.

1What is a tessellation?

A tessellation (or tiling) is a way of covering the whole flat plane with copies of a shape so that there are no gaps and no overlaps. Think of bathroom tiles.

Our tiles live on the square grid: every tile is a small polyomino — a block of grid cells glued together. A valid tiling covers every cell exactly once.

The tricky part is that the copies are allowed to be rotated or flipped, not just slid around. That freedom is what makes finding tilings a puzzle.

2D4 — the eight poses of a square

Before tiles, think about a single shape. A square has 8 symmetries: ways to move it that land it exactly back on itself. Those 8 moves form a group called D4.

They are:

  • 4 rotations: do nothing (ID), a quarter turn (R90), a half turn (R180), three-quarters (R270).
  • 4 reflections: flip left-right (FX), flip up-down (FY), flip across the two diagonals (TR, TL).
A group is just a collection of moves where any two moves done one after the other is again a move in the collection, and every move can be undone. D4 has 8 members, so we say its order is 8.

Two smaller groups show up a lot: C4, the 4 rotations only, and C2, just ID and R180.

3Symmetry of a tile

The set of moves that leave the tile unchanged is called its symmetry group (or stabiliser). The number of genuinely different poses is 8 ÷ (size of the symmetry group). This is the orbit–stabiliser idea: the bigger the symmetry group, the fewer different poses.

4Lattices — the rhythm of the pattern

When you tile the plane, the copies repeat on a regular grid called a lattice. A lattice is built from two arrows, b1 and b2: every lattice point is a whole-number combination m·b1 + n·b2.

A rectangle, or a centred rectangle?

The parallelogram spanned by b1 and b2 is the fundamental cell. Its area (minus lines) is the covolume: how many cells of the plane are in one repeat. A tile's area must divide the covolume to have any chance of tiling.

5Holohedry — the lattice's own symmetry

A lattice can look symmetric too. The set of D4 moves that map a lattice onto itself is its holohedry. This is a property of the grid of repeating points, not of the tiles sitting on it.

The same chosen lattice, tested against all 8 moves:

A square lattice has holohedry D4. A plain rectangle is only D2. A centred rectangle — a rectangle with an extra point in its middle — also has D2, but its sides are not at right angles the way you'd first guess, so we detect it by the holohedry rather than by eyeballing angles.

A tiling can have less symmetry than its lattice. The lattice's holohedry is the most symmetry it could have; the tiling's point group is what it actually uses.

6Symmetry of a whole tiling

Now shrink back out and look at the full pattern. Its symmetries are the moves that leave the entire tiling unchanged.

MoveWhat it does
TranslationSlides the pattern. The set of slides that work is its translation lattice.
RotationSpins the pattern about a point (usually 180° or 90° on our grid).
MirrorReflects it across a line; the line is called a mirror line.
GlideA reflection plus a half-step slide along the line. It looks like a mirror at a glance, but no single line reflects the pattern.

The mirror-vs-glide difference is exactly why we have separate names pm (has a mirror) and pg (only a glide). To tell them apart we ask: can the reflection be moved onto itself by a slide that cancels its sideways shift? If yes it's a real mirror; if not, it's a glide.

7Wallpaper groups

In total there are exactly 17 ways a pattern can repeat forever — the 17 wallpaper groups. On the square grid only 12 of them can appear.

Reading the name

The names are a compact recipe:

  • The first letter is the lattice: p = primitive (plain), c = centred (a point in the middle of the cell).
  • The rest lists the symmetry in each main direction: 1 = none, 2 = 180° turn, 4 = 90° turn, m = mirror, g = glide.

So pmm = primitive, mirror in direction 1, mirror in direction 2. pmg = primitive, mirror one way, glide the other. p4g = the square grid, 4-fold spins, glides along the sides.

The point group (like C2 or D4) tells you which rotations and flips are involved; it says nothing about where their centres or mirror lines sit. The full wallpaper group adds that "where" — which is why two patterns can share point group D2 yet be pmm versus cmm.

Fun fact: the same tile can make different wallpaper groups! Drawing an L-tromino (3 cells) the Lab finds both a cm and a cmm tiling, plus p2, pg and others.

8Is it really a tiling?

Checking every cell of an infinite plane sounds impossible. Two simple facts make it easy.

  1. Area check. One repeat of the pattern (the fundamental cell) contains a fixed number of grid cells. The tile area times the number of copies must equal exactly that number. If not, there must be a gap or an overlap.
  2. Overlap check. If we also prove that no two copies overlap, then the copies can't leave a gap — because the average coverage already works out to exactly 1 everywhere.

Overlaps are found by comparing each pair of tiles: shift one so their lattice positions line up and see whether any cells coincide. That is fast and exact, so the program never has to look at the whole plane.

9When are two tilings "the same"?

If you slide or rotate the whole pattern, you haven't made a new tiling — just described the same one differently. We want to count it once.

Two descriptions are the same tiling when we can:

  • slide one onto the other, and
  • possibly rotate or flip the whole thing,

so that every tile lands exactly on a tile.

We turn each tiling into a canonical signature: a single string computed from the set of tile shapes (their actual cells, not any arbitrary labels). Two tilings are the same exactly when their signatures match. Doing this also quietly fixes two sneaky cases:

  • a shape that is itself symmetric can be written two ways (the labels differ but the tiles don't);
  • a pattern can be described with a bigger repeating cell than necessary — we shrink it to the smallest one first.
